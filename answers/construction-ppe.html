What PPE does OSHA require on a construction site?

Short answer: OSHA does not hand construction one fixed PPE list for every site. PPE is hazard-based: under 29 CFR 1926.95(a), protective equipment for the eyes, face, head, and extremities, protective clothing, respiratory devices, and protective shields and barriers must be provided, used, and maintained in a sanitary and reliable condition wherever hazards make it necessary — and under 1926.28(a) the employer is responsible for requiring appropriate PPE in all operations where there is exposure to hazardous conditions. Subpart E then sets the specific rules: helmets under 1926.100, safety-toe footwear under 1926.96, eye and face protection under 1926.102, hearing protection under 1926.101, respiratory protection under 1926.103, and life jackets for work over or near water under 1926.106. Fall protection lives mainly in Subpart M.

Subpart E, section by section

1926.95 — the general PPE rule. PPE must be provided, used, and maintained wherever process, environmental, chemical, radiological, or mechanical hazards can injure a worker through absorption, inhalation, or physical contact. PPE must be of safe design and construction for the work. Under 1926.95(d), PPE used to comply with Part 1926 is provided by the employer at no cost to employees, with stated exceptions such as non-specialty safety-toe footwear and everyday clothing.

1926.100 — head protection. Employees working where there is possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock and burns must be protected by protective helmets. That describes most active jobsites, which is why the hard hat is the default.

1926.96 — foot protection. Safety-toe footwear for employees must meet the safety-toe footwear standard cited in the rule. In practice, buy footwear labeled to the current safety-toe standard and rated for the impact, compression, and puncture hazards on your site.

1926.102 — eye and face protection. Employees must be provided eye and face protection when machines or operations present potential eye or face injury from physical, chemical, or radiation agents — grinding, cutting, chipping, welding, and chemical handling are the everyday examples. Protection has to match the hazard, including side protection and filter lenses where radiant energy is involved.

1926.101 — hearing protection. Where it is not feasible to reduce noise levels or exposure duration to the permissible limits in Table D-2 of 1926.52, ear protective devices must be provided and used, and ear inserts must be fitted or determined individually by competent persons. Plain cotton is not an acceptable protective device.

1926.103 — respiratory protection. The requirements for construction are identical to 29 CFR 1910.134: when respirators are necessary to protect health, the employer runs a written program covering selection, medical evaluation, fit testing, use, cleaning and storage, training, and program evaluation. Dust masks handed out with no program do not satisfy this where a respirator is actually required — and silica, lead, and asbestos each carry their own construction standards on top of it.

1926.104 and 1926.105 — belts, lifelines, nets. Safety belts, lifelines, and lanyards are to be used only for employee safeguarding, and safety nets are covered here too — but the duty to provide fall protection, and the systems that count, are in Subpart M starting at 1926.501. Read both before you hang a harness program on Subpart E alone.

1926.106 — work over or near water. Where a drowning danger exists, employees get U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jackets or buoyant work vests, with ring buoys and a lifesaving skiff immediately available.

Hands and hi-vis. Appropriate gloves fall under the general duty of 1926.95 and 1926.28 for the hazard at hand — cuts, chemicals, electrical work, and heat each call for a different glove. High-visibility clothing is required for flaggers and workers exposed to public vehicular traffic under the traffic-control rules, and most sites require it across the board as policy even where the federal rule is narrower.

What this means on a real site

Walk the job and list the hazards first — overhead work, cutting and grinding, noise, dust, chemicals, traffic, water. The hazard list writes the PPE list. Then make sure the gear is actually there, actually fits, and is actually worn: a PPE rule on paper with helmets riding in truck cabs is the citation OSHA writes most. Replace damaged gear, train the crew on what each piece is for, and restock consumables — plugs, gloves, lenses — before the box runs empty.

Common questions

Does OSHA require hard hats on all construction sites?
1926.100(a) requires protective helmets wherever there is possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock and burns. Most active sites qualify — the rule follows the hazard.

Who pays for PPE?
The employer, at no cost to employees, for PPE used to comply with Part 1926 — 1926.95(d), with exceptions listed in the rule for items like non-specialty safety-toe footwear and everyday clothing.

When is a respirator required?
When it is necessary to protect employee health — and then 1926.103 applies the full 1910.134 program: written program, medical evaluation, fit testing, and training.

Is fall protection in Subpart E?
Partly — belts, lifelines, and nets sit at 1926.104–105 — but the main fall protection duties are Subpart M, starting at 1926.501.

This page is a plain-language summary of the federal rules, not legal advice. State-plan states can have their own rules — check yours.
