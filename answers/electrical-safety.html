Do construction sites need GFCI protection?

Short answer: Yes — on every temporary receptacle your crew actually uses. Under 29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1)(ii), all 120-volt, single-phase, 15- and 20-ampere receptacle outlets on a construction site that are not part of the permanent wiring and are in use by employees must have approved GFCI protection. The only alternative in 1926.404(b)(1)(i) is a written assured equipment grounding conductor program — there is no third option of doing neither.

Subpart K (29 CFR 1926.400–449) is OSHA's construction electrical standard. Temporary power is its center of gravity: cords run through water, mud, rebar, and traffic all day, which is why the standard protects the receptacle, the cord, and the work practice at the same time.

GFCI or assured grounding — pick one, in writing either way: 1926.404(b)(1)

GFCI is the default. A GFCI trips when current leaking to ground differs from current returning by about 5 milliamps — in as little as 1/40 of a second. Put it at the receptacle, in the cord, or at the breaker, but every covered outlet in use needs it. One narrow exception: receptacles on a two-wire, single-phase portable or vehicle-mounted generator rated not more than 5kW, where the generator conductors are insulated from the frame and all other grounded surfaces, need not be GFCI-protected.

The assured program is paperwork plus testing. It must cover all cord sets, receptacles not part of the permanent wiring, and cord-and-plug equipment; have a written description available at the jobsite; designate a competent person; require daily visual inspections for damage; and require continuity and terminal-attachment tests before first use, after repairs, after any incident that may have caused damage, and at least every 3 months (every 6 months for fixed equipment not exposed to damage) — with records.

Guarding live parts at 50 volts or more — 1926.403

Enclose it or isolate it. Live parts of electric equipment operating at 50 volts or more must be guarded against accidental contact by approved enclosures, or by location, partitions, or screens that keep unqualified people away. Entrances to spaces with exposed live parts carry conspicuous warning signs — unqualified persons stay out.

Working space counts too. Sufficient access and working space must be provided and maintained around electric equipment to allow ready and safe operation and maintenance — a panel buried behind stacked material fails that test before anyone touches a wire.

No contact, no work — 1926.416(a), and cords: 1926.416(e)

Deenergize and ground it, or guard it effectively. Under 1926.416(a)(1), no employee may work close enough to any part of an electric power circuit to contact it in the course of work unless protected by deenergizing the circuit and grounding it, or by guarding it effectively by insulation or other means. Before work begins, the employer must ascertain — by inquiry, direct observation, or instruments — whether any part of an energized circuit, exposed or concealed, is located where work could bring a person, tool, or machine into contact with it.

Worn or frayed cords are out. Under 1926.416(e)(1), worn or frayed electric cords or cables shall not be used, and under 1926.416(e)(2) flexible cords may not be fastened with staples, hung in a way that damages the outer jacket or insulation, or used as a substitute for fixed wiring. Lockout and tagging of circuits — 1926.417

Tag the controls. Controls that are to be deactivated during work on energized or deenergized equipment or circuits shall be tagged under 1926.417(a).

Render it inoperative, tag every energizing point. Under 1926.417(b), equipment or circuits that are deenergized shall be rendered inoperative and shall have tags attached at all points where the equipment or circuits can be energized. Under 1926.417(c), the tags must identify plainly the equipment or circuits being worked on — a tag nobody can read protects nobody.

Common questions

Does a GFCI breaker in the temp panel cover the whole spider box?
If every covered receptacle downstream is actually protected by it, yes — protection is judged at the outlet in use, not by where the device sits. Test at the receptacle; a breaker that does not protect the outlet you are plugged into does not count for that outlet.

Can we just use the assured grounding program and skip GFCIs?
Yes — but only as the full written program in 1926.404(b)(1)(iii): written description on site, competent person, daily inspections, and documented tests on the required schedule. A verbal "we check our cords" is not the program.

What about the small-generator exception?
It is narrow: two-wire, single-phase, portable or vehicle-mounted, rated not more than 5kW, with circuit conductors insulated from the frame and all other grounded surfaces. Miss any condition and GFCI protection is required.
