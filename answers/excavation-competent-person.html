What does an excavation competent person actually do?

Short answer: Under 29 CFR 1926.650(b), the excavation competent person is the one person on the job who is capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards in and around the excavation and has authorization to take prompt corrective measures to eliminate them. Both halves are mandatory. A sharp operator with no authority to stop the job is not a competent person under the rule, and neither is a superintendent with authority who cannot read soil.

Subpart P (29 CFR 1926.650–652) hangs more duties on this one role than almost any other standard in construction: inspections, soil classification, air testing, water control, and the call that empties the trench. This page is that job description, in the order the workday runs.

The definition — capability plus authority: 1926.650(b)

Two tests, both must pass. The person must be able to spot the hazards Subpart P cares about — cave-in signs, failing protective systems, bad air, water, surcharge loads, nearby structures and traffic — and must be able to act on what they see immediately, without calling the office. If the fix needs permission from someone off site, the authority half fails.

There is no OSHA competent-person certificate. The standard names no license, course, or card. OSHA judges the role by capability: the employer has to pick someone whose training and experience actually let them identify excavation hazards, and give that person real stop-work authority. Paper titles do not substitute for either.

Daily inspections — before work, through the shift, after rain: 1926.651(k)

Inspect before anyone gets in. Under 1926.651(k)(1), the competent person inspects the excavation, the adjacent areas, and the protective systems daily, prior to the start of work, and as needed throughout the shift — looking for evidence of possible cave-ins, indications of failure of protective systems, hazardous atmospheres, or other hazardous conditions. The duty applies whenever employee exposure can be reasonably anticipated.

Events restart the clock. A fresh inspection is required after every rainstorm or other hazard-increasing occurrence — heavy vibration from traffic or compaction, a broken water line, freeze-and-thaw, or deepening the cut into a new soil layer all qualify. Yesterday's good inspection says nothing about this morning's walls.

A bad finding empties the hole. Under 1926.651(k)(2), where the inspection turns up evidence of a possible cave-in, protective-system failure, hazardous atmosphere, or other hazardous condition, exposed employees are removed from the hazardous area until the necessary precautions have been taken. That removal is not a suggestion the crew can vote on — it is the competent person's core power, and the reason the definition demands authority.

Soil classification — one visual test, one manual test: Appendix A

The classification picks the slope. The competent person classifies the soil as stable rock, Type A, Type B, or Type C, and that call sets the maximum allowable slope under Appendix B — 3/4:1 (53°) for Type A, 1:1 (45°) for Type B, 1-1/2:1 (34°) for Type C — or the benching and shoring tables the crew may use. A wrong call here is a wrong protective system everywhere else.

Two kinds of evidence, minimum. Appendix A requires at least one visual analysis and at least one manual analysis. Visual: read the faces and spoil for fissures, slumping, bulging or heaving at the bottom, layers dipping into the cut, and water seepage. Manual: test strength by hand or simple instrument — the plasticity (thread) test, dry strength, thumb penetration, or a pocket penetrometer. When conditions change, reclassify — soil that was Type B on Monday can be Type C after Tuesday's rain.

Air, water, and the rest of the competent person's list: 1926.651(c), (g), (h)

Test the air where bad air is possible. Under 1926.651(g)(1), in excavations deeper than 4 feet where an oxygen-deficient atmosphere (less than 19.5 percent oxygen) or another hazardous atmosphere exists or could reasonably be expected — near chemical storage, in landfill ground, in or near backfilled areas — the atmosphere is tested before employees enter and as often as necessary during the shift. Where a hazardous atmosphere exists or may develop, rescue equipment — breathing apparatus, a safety harness and line, or a basket stretcher — must be readily available under 1926.651(g)(2)(i).

Water is the competent person's watch too. Employees do not work in excavations with accumulated water unless adequate precautions are taken, and where water is controlled or removed by pumping, the competent person monitors the water removal equipment and operations under 1926.651(h)(2). Excavations subject to runoff from heavy rains require a competent-person inspection under 1926.651(h)(3) before work resumes.

Ramps and the under-5-foot call. Structural ramps used solely by employees for access or egress are designed by a competent person under 1926.651(c)(1)(i) — equipment ramps take a registered professional engineer. And the only route to working without a protective system in a cut under 5 feet deep is the competent person's examination showing no indication of a potential cave-in under 1926.652(a)(1)(ii). At 5 feet or deeper a protective system is required unless the cut is entirely in stable rock, and past 20 feet the system must be designed by a registered professional engineer.

The everyday numbers still apply around all of this: egress within 25 feet of lateral travel in trenches 4 feet or deeper (1926.651(c)(2)), and spoil, materials, and equipment kept at least 2 feet back from the edge (1926.651(j)(2)). Common questions

Does the competent person need a certification card?
No. Subpart P defines the role by capability and authority, not by a credential. OSHA has clarified the standard sets no specific training requirement — but the employer must be able to show the person chosen can actually identify excavation hazards and has the authority to correct them on the spot.

Can the competent person also work in the trench?
The standard does not forbid it, but the inspection duties under 1926.651(k) — before work, as needed throughout the shift, and after every rainstorm or hazard-increasing occurrence — still have to happen, and the person must be free to pull the crew out the moment a hazard shows up.

Does every excavation need a competent person, even a shallow one?
The inspection duty in 1926.651(k) is written for excavations generally, whenever employee exposure can be reasonably anticipated — and the under-5-foot exception in 1926.652(a)(1)(ii) exists only on the strength of a competent person's examination finding no indication of a potential cave-in. Shallow does not mean nobody is in charge of the call.
