How close can a crane get to power lines?

Short answer: Pick one of three options in 29 CFR 1926.1408(a) before work starts — deenergize the line and visibly ground it at the worksite (confirmed by the utility owner/operator), keep every part of the equipment, load line, and load at least 20 feet away, or stay outside the Table A minimum clearance for the line's voltage: 10 feet up to 50 kV, 15 feet over 50 to 200 kV, 20 feet over 200 to 350 kV. Until the utility confirms otherwise, every power line is presumed energized.

Subpart CC (29 CFR 1926.1400–1442) is OSHA's cranes and derricks standard for construction. Power-line contact is its deadliest recurring scenario, which is why the standard regulates the clearance, the operator, the signal person, the inspections, and the ground under the machine as one system.

Power-line safety — the three options: 1926.1408

Option 1 — deenergize and ground. The utility owner/operator confirms the line has been deenergized and that it is visibly grounded at the worksite. A phone promise is not enough — the grounding has to be visible where the work happens.

Option 2 — the 20-foot rule. No part of the equipment, load line, or load — including rigging and lifting accessories — gets closer than 20 feet to the power line, with the encroachment-prevention measures in 1926.1408(b) in place.

Option 3 — Table A clearance. Minimum clearance distances by voltage (nominal, kV, alternating current): up to 50 kV — 10 feet; over 50 to 200 kV — 15 feet; over 200 to 350 kV — 20 feet; over 350 to 500 kV — 25 feet; over 500 to 750 kV — 35 feet; over 750 to 1,000 kV — 45 feet; over 1,000 kV — as established by the utility owner/operator or a registered professional engineer who is a qualified person for electrical power transmission and distribution.

Working inside the trigger distance means planning first. When Option 2 or 3 is used, the employer must hold a planning meeting with the operator and crew in the work area, use nonconductive tag lines, and erect and maintain an elevated warning line, barricade, or line of signs with flags at the clearance boundary — and if the operator cannot see that warning line, a dedicated spotter who meets the signal-person qualifications must watch the separation and stay in communication with the operator.

Operator certification and evaluation — 1926.1427

Certified for the machine, evaluated by the employer. The employer must ensure each operator is certified or licensed for the type and capacity of equipment they run — by an accredited testing organization, a government licensing authority, an audited employer program, or the U.S. military — and must separately evaluate the operator on the size and configuration of the equipment they will actually operate, then document that evaluation. Certification alone does not put an unfamiliar operator on a different machine without the evaluation.

Trainees stay supervised. An operator in training may only operate under the direct supervision of a trainer who meets the standard's requirements, and never on work the standard bars for trainees, such as lifts near power lines or hoisting personnel.

Signal persons — when required, and qualified how: 1926.1428

Required on blind work. A signal person is required when the point of operation is not in full view of the operator, when the operator's view is obstructed in the direction of travel, or when the operator or the person handling the load decides site conditions call for one.

Qualified means tested and documented. The signal person must know the signal types in use, be competent applying them, understand equipment operation and crane dynamics, and know the signal requirements of the standard — demonstrated through an oral or written test plus a practical test, with documentation from a third-party qualified evaluator or the employer's qualified evaluator available at the worksite. Signals themselves are governed by 1926.1419–1422: standard hand signals, voice signals, or new signals only by agreement, and a stop signal from anyone must be obeyed.

Inspections — shift, monthly, annual: 1926.1412

Every shift, before use. A competent person inspects the equipment at the start of each shift — controls, safety devices, hooks, wire rope, and the rest of the shift checklist — and the equipment is not used until it is determined to be in safe operating condition.

Monthly, documented for 3 months. The monthly inspection covers the same items and must be documented with the date, the inspector's name and signature, and the equipment's serial number or identifier; keep the documentation at least 3 months.

Annual, comprehensive, by a qualified person. The annual inspection is documented and kept at least 12 months. Add inspections after any modification or repair of a safety-related part, and before use after the equipment has sat idle 3 months or more. Ground conditions and assembly/disassembly — 1926.1402, 1926.1404

The ground is part of the lift plan. Equipment may not be assembled or used unless ground conditions are firm, drained, and graded to a level surface where necessary, meeting the manufacturer's specifications for support and level. The controlling entity must tell the equipment user and the assembly/disassembly director about hazards under the set-up area — voids, tanks, utilities — and where they are.

A qualified A/D director runs assembly and disassembly. Under 1926.1404, assembly and disassembly are directed by a qualified person who reviews procedures, controls crew movements, and stops when conditions — including wind beyond the manufacturer's limits — make the work unsafe. Nobody works under a boom being raised or lowered except as the standard allows.

Common questions

Does the 20-foot rule apply to the load and rigging, or just the boom?
Everything. Under 1926.1408(a)(2), no part of the equipment, load line, or load — expressly including rigging and lifting accessories — may get closer than the chosen clearance. A boom at 25 feet with a load swinging to 12 feet is a violation.

Can the crew assume a line is dead because work looks finished nearby?
No. Power lines are presumed energized unless the utility owner/operator confirms the line has been deenergized and it is visibly grounded at the worksite. Appearance is never the test.

Does operator certification transfer between crane types?
Certification is by type and capacity, and the employer's evaluation is on the size and configuration the operator will run. A new machine type or configuration means a new evaluation before operating it.
