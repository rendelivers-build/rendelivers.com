How are steel joists erected under OSHA?

Short answer: A steel joist stays on the crane until it is field-bolted at both seats and restrained at the bottom chord. Under 29 CFR 1926.757(a)(1), each column gets a stabilizer plate at least 6 by 6 inches, extending at least 3 inches below the bottom chord with a 13/16-inch hole — and where Tables A and B require erection bridging, the cables stay on until that bolted diagonal bridging is installed and anchored.

This page is the joist deep-dive to our steel erection answer page. Subpart R treats joists separately from beams and columns because an unbridged joist can roll — most of 1926.757 is about keeping it from doing exactly that, in span order.

Stabilizing the column — the first joist does two jobs: 1926.757(a)

Field-bolted at the column. Where columns are not framed in at least two directions with solid web members, a joist is field-bolted at the column to give the column lateral stability during erection. Bottom chords are stabilized against rotation, and a joist is never placed on a support structure that is not itself stabilized. Landed joists are secured against unintentional displacement before installation, and no strength-affecting modification happens without the project structural engineer of record's approval.

The 60-foot split. Joists at or near columns spanning 60 feet or less must be designed strong enough for one employee to release the hoisting cable without erection bridging. Joists spanning more than 60 feet are set in tandem with all bridging installed — unless a qualified person designs an equivalent method into the site-specific erection plan. Joists and joist girders are never fall arrest anchorages without a qualified person's written approval.

Attachment — two welds or two bolts, every end: 1926.757(b)

K-series: each end attached with at least two 1/8-inch fillet welds 1 inch long, or two 1/2-inch bolts, or the equivalent. LH and DLH series and joist girders: at least two 1/4-inch fillet welds 2 inches long, or two 3/4-inch bolts, or the equivalent.

Attach before you move on. Each joist is attached at least at one end, on both sides of the seat, immediately upon placement and before additional joists are placed. Pre-assembled panels are attached at each corner before the hoisting cables come off. In bays of 40 feet or more, individual joist connections must be fabricated to allow field bolting during erection.

Erection bridging — by span, from Tables A and B: 1926.757(c)–(d)

Terminus first. A bridging terminus point is established before bridging is installed; if permanent terminus points cannot be used during erection, temporary ones are required. Bridging placement follows the erection drawing — the drawing is the exclusive indicator — and bridging attachments never protrude above the top chord.

At or past the Table A/B span: one row of bolted diagonal erection bridging near midspan, cables on until it is installed and anchored, and no more than one employee on the span until all other bridging is in. Over 60 through 100 feet: all rows bolted diagonal, two rows near the third points, no more than two employees until all bridging is in. Over 100 through 144 feet: cables stay on until all bridging is installed and anchored. Over 144 feet: erection follows the beams-and-columns rules at 1926.756.

Landing loads — nothing goes up early: 1926.757(e)

No construction loads on joists until all bridging is installed and anchored and all joist-bearing ends are attached, and any load placed must not exceed any joist's carrying capacity. A bridging bundle caps at 1,000 pounds, sits on at least three joists secured at one end, within 1 foot of the secured end.

Decking bundles are the narrow exception: up to 4,000 pounds, on at least three joists attached at both ends, with at least one row of bridging installed and anchored — and only after a qualified person has documented in the site-specific erection plan that the structure can support the load. The edge of any construction load sits within 1 foot of the bearing surface of the joist end.

Systems-engineered metal buildings — the companion rule: 1926.758

Four anchor bolts per column, and rigid frames need 50 percent of their bolts installed and tightened on both sides of the web adjacent to each flange before the load line is released. Both ends of every joist must be fully bolted and/or welded before cables are released, anyone gets on the joists, or any construction load goes on them. No construction loads on framework that is not safely bolted, welded, or otherwise secured — and purlins and girts are not fall arrest anchorages without a qualified person's written approval. Purlins become a walking/working surface only after all permanent bridging is installed and fall protection is provided.

Common questions

Can one connector walk an unbridged joist to release the cable?
Only within the limits of 1926.757: joists at or near columns at 60 feet or less are designed for one employee to release the cable without erection bridging, and on joists that do not require erection bridging under Tables A and B, only one employee is allowed on the joist until all bridging is installed and anchored.

Is a joist a legal tie-off point?
No — not by default. Under 1926.757(a)(9), steel joists and joist girders may not be used as fall arrest anchorage points unless written approval is obtained from a qualified person. The same written-approval rule covers purlins and girts under 1926.758.

Does the two-bolt beam rule cover joists too?
No. The two-wrench-tight-bolt rule at 1926.756(a)(1) governs solid web members. Joists have their own attachment minimums at 1926.757(b) — weld/bolt sizes by series — plus the field-bolted seat and stabilizer-plate conditions before cable release.
