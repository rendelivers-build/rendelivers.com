What should a contractor estimate include?

Short answer: A contractor estimate should include the contractor and client information and project address; a written scope of work that says what is included and what is excluded; line items for labor, materials, equipment, subcontractors, permits and fees, and disposal; allowances for anything the client has not picked yet; overhead and markup built into the number; the total price; the estimated start date and duration; the payment schedule; written change-order terms; warranty terms; and an expiration date for the price, with signature lines. An estimate missing any of those is where money arguments are born.

The checklist, item by item

1. Who and where. Your business name and contact information, the client's name, and the project address. Jobs get estimated for the wrong house more often than anybody admits.

2. Scope of work — in writing. What you will do, in plain tasks: demo, framing, rough-in, finish, cleanup. Then the part most guys skip: exclusions. What you will not do — permits pulled by owner, painting by others, landscaping not included. Exclusions written down are protection; exclusions assumed are a fight.

3. Line-item costs. Break the price into labor, materials, equipment, subcontractor quotes, permits and fees, and dump or disposal. Line items let a client compare two bids honestly — and let you defend yours. One lump number with nothing behind it loses to the cheaper lump number every time.

4. Allowances. Tile, fixtures, cabinets — anything the client has not selected gets a stated allowance amount. When the selection comes in over the allowance, the difference is a documented adjustment, not a surprise.

5. Overhead and markup. Whether you show it or build it in, the number has to carry the business: fuel, insurance, office time, and profit. An estimate that covers only labor and materials is a job you paid to work on.

6. Total and what can change it. The total price, stated plainly, plus the conditions that can move it: hidden damage, client changes, price holds.

7. Schedule. Estimated start date and duration, and what the schedule depends on — permits, materials, weather, inspections.

8. Payment terms. Deposit, progress draws tied to completed stages, and final payment on completion. Money terms agreed before the job are professional; money terms invented during the job are a dispute.

9. Change orders. One sentence that saves careers: extra work is priced and approved in writing before it is done. Verbal change orders are how contractors work for free.

10. Warranty, expiration, signatures. What you warranty and for how long, how long the price is good, and a place for both parties to sign. Material prices move; an estimate with no expiration date is a promise you did not mean to make.

Estimate, quote, or bid?

An estimate is an approximation that can move when conditions change. A quote or bid is a firmer offer on defined work. Guys use the words interchangeably, so the document has to carry the weight: scope, exclusions, allowances, and terms written on the page are what protect you — not the word at the top.

The three things that cause most estimate blowups: a vague scope, allowances that were never priced, and change orders that were never written down. Fix those three and most jobs end with a handshake instead of an argument.

Common questions

What is the difference between an estimate, a quote, and a bid?
An estimate approximates cost and can move; a quote or bid is a firmer price on defined work. Either way, the written scope, exclusions, and terms on the document are what control.

Should an estimate show markup?
It does not have to be its own line — many contractors build it into their prices. But overhead and profit have to be in the number somewhere, every time, or the job loses money.

What causes the most disputes over estimates?
Vague scope, unpriced allowances, and verbal change orders. Write all three down and most disputes never happen.
