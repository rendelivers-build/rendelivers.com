What must you do about underground utilities before digging?

Home Apparel Tools Catalog Safety Gear Shop by Trade Templates Software & Literature Books Gift Guide

Short answer: Before the bucket touches ground, two paragraphs run the job. Under 29 CFR 1926.651(b), the estimated location of underground utilities must be determined before opening the excavation, the utility companies or owners must be contacted and asked to establish the locations before actual excavation starts, the exact location must be found by safe and acceptable means as digging approaches it, and exposed installations must be protected, supported, or removed while the excavation is open. Under 1926.651(i), a structure whose stability the digging endangers gets shoring, bracing, or underpinning — and you do not dig below a footing except under the four narrow exceptions in (i)(2).

In the U.S., the customary contact route in 1926.651(b)(2) is the state one-call center — dial 811 — but the federal text states the duty, not the phone number: contact within established or customary local response times, before the work starts.

Before you open it — the locate duties: 1926.651(b)(1)–(2)

Estimated location first. Paragraph (b)(1) covers sewer, telephone, fuel, electric, and water lines, plus any other underground installation reasonably expected to be encountered. Its location must be determined prior to opening the excavation — records, plans, and surface evidence, before digging, not after the first strike.

Then contact the owners. Paragraph (b)(2) requires the utility companies or owners to be contacted within established or customary local response times, advised of the proposed work, and asked to establish the locations before actual excavation starts. State and local law often sets a longer, specific notice window — the federal 24-hour figure below is a fallback, not a scheduling target.

The no-response fallback. If the company or owner cannot respond within 24 hours (unless state or local law requires a longer period), or cannot establish the exact location, the employer may proceed — provided it does so with caution and uses detection equipment or other acceptable means to locate the installations. Proceeding blind is not one of the options the paragraph offers.

As you approach, and while it is open: 1926.651(b)(3)–(4)

Exact location, by safe means. Paragraph (b)(3): when excavation operations approach the estimated location, the exact location shall be determined by safe and acceptable means — in practice, careful hand digging or vacuum excavation inside the tolerance zone rather than machine digging through a mark.

Protect what you expose. Paragraph (b)(4): while the excavation is open, underground installations shall be protected, supported, or removed as necessary to safeguard employees. An exposed line left hanging unsupported across a trench is a (b)(4) problem even if nobody has hit it.

The building next door — adjacent structures: 1926.651(i)

Support it before it moves. Paragraph (i)(1): where excavation operations endanger the stability of adjoining buildings, walls, or other structures, support systems such as shoring, bracing, or underpinning shall be provided to ensure the structure's stability for the protection of employees.

Sidewalks and pavements count too. Paragraph (i)(3): sidewalks, pavements, and appurtenant structures shall not be undermined unless a support system or another method of protection protects employees from their possible collapse.

The daily check covers the neighbors. The competent person's daily inspections under 1926.651(k) cover the excavation, the adjacent areas, and the protective systems — cracks, settlement, or a support system showing distress are stop-and-fix findings, with exposed employees removed under (k)(2) until precautions are taken.

Below a footing — the four exceptions only: 1926.651(i)(2)

Excavation below the level of the base or footing of a foundation or retaining wall that could reasonably be expected to pose a hazard is not permitted except when one of four conditions holds: (i) a support system such as underpinning is provided to ensure employee safety and the structure's stability; (ii) the excavation is in stable rock; (iii) a registered professional engineer has approved the determination that the structure is sufficiently removed from the excavation to be unaffected by it; or (iv) a registered professional engineer has approved the determination that the excavation work will not pose a hazard to employees. "It looks far enough away" is not an exception — two of the four routes run through a registered professional engineer.

Common questions

Does OSHA itself require calling 811?
The federal text at 1926.651(b)(2) does not name 811. It requires contacting utility companies or owners within established or customary local response times and asking them to establish the locations before excavation starts. In most states the one-call center (811) is that customary route, and state law sets the actual notice period.

The locate marks are wrong — who is covered?
Paragraph (b)(3) is why marks are only the start: as operations approach the estimated location, the exact location must be determined by safe and acceptable means. Digging mechanically through a marked zone on faith meets neither (b)(3) nor the caution condition in (b)(2).

Can we undercut the sidewalk to reach the line?
Not unprotected. Under 1926.651(i)(3), sidewalks, pavements, and appurtenant structures shall not be undermined unless a support system or another method of protection protects employees from possible collapse.
